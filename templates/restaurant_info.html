The tableOne table, one sitting, on the nights the kitchen is open. Residents are seated first.

Dinner at the château, cooked by our own chefs and served in the dining room by candlelight.

Meals are arranged in advance rather than served to order, so that the kitchen buys for the table it is cooking for. Lunch runs on the same terms, and is often carried out to the terrace.

There is no fixed menu. What you eat depends on the Sunday market in the village, on the producers the chef buys from, and in summer on the château gardens.

Where It Comes From

Most of it comes from within a few miles.

Not every night The kitchen does not cook every evening{% if settings and settings['closed_day'] %} — it is closed on {{ settings['closed_day'] }}{% endif %}. We will tell you which nights are running when you book, and where to eat in the village on the ones that are not.

Bread and pastries come from the boulangerie in Les Cabannes, three minutes' walk below the gates, and arrive while they are still warm. The Sunday morning market in the village square supplies most of the rest — vegetables, cheeses from the mountain farms, and charcuterie.

The château gardens give herbs through the year, and in summer cherries, plums and raspberries. Trout comes from the Ariège. Lamb comes from the Pyrénées, sometimes from the valley visible out of the dining room windows.

La Salle à Manger

The room in which dinner is served was among the most damaged in the château.

Its ceiling had gone. What remained of the panelling was under later paint, and the floor beneath it had rotted through in places. It took several years to bring back — the plaster consolidated rather than replaced, the panelling cleaned by hand, and a new floor laid in oak beneath walls left deliberately as they were found.

Dinner is served by candlelight in the dining room. In winter the fire is lit.

The kitchen was the first room brought back

It was recovered before the bedrooms, before the salons, and before anything else that guests now see. This house was organised around its table from the year it was built.

The room you dine in is protected under Class I — the plaster stays as the centuries left it. The table, the linen, the floor beneath your chair and the kitchen behind you are all new. The imperfection is deliberate. You will eat extremely well in a room that is not finished.

Through the Day

Where You Will Eat

No two days use the same room. That is deliberate — there are ninety-four of them.

Morning

Breakfast

Bread from the village, cheeses, charcuterie, fruit from the garden in season, and coffee that keeps arriving. Inside, or on the terrace if the weather allows.

Midday

Lunch, wherever

A picnic carried into the parkland, a long table in the gardens, or the Salle à Manger if it is raining. Often whatever came back from the market that morning.

Four o'clock

Afternoon tea

A Marie Antoinette-inspired tea, laid properly. Tom is as serious about the sweet as the savoury, and the pastry side of this kitchen shows it.

Seven

Aperitifs in the Renaissance kitchen

The oldest working room in the building, with a drink in your hand before dinner. It is the room most guests remember.

Half past seven

Dinner

By candlelight in the dining room, or en plein air under the sky if the evening is warm. Family style, plated, or laid out to help yourself — it depends on the night.

Occasionally

A banquet in the Salon de Musique

An eighteenth-century banquet in the music room, for the nights that warrant one. It has been known to end in dancing.

The 1750s

This Table Has Form

When the château was new it was written of as the place to be wined and dined in the Ariège. Aristocrats made the journey. So did Voltaire and Diderot.

The room they came for is the room dinner is served in. Two hundred and seventy years is a long time for a house to keep a reputation for its table, and it is not one we intend to be the generation that loses.

It was the place to be wined and dined.

Of Gudanes in the 1750s

Provenance

Named, Not Gestured At

Almost none of it travels far. These are the actual places it comes from.

Two minutes

La Ferme du Quié

The farm shop at Les Cabannes, named for the mountain above the village. Local wine, cheese, ham and condiments — a two-minute walk from the front gate.

Sunday

The market at Les Cabannes

Three hundred and seventeen people live in the village. The Sunday market is where the kitchen buys, and where the chef has bought from the same producers for twenty years.

Wednesday and Saturday

Tarascon-sur-Ariège

Twenty minutes down the valley and considerably bigger. Worth the drive when the kitchen is cooking for a full table.

Summer

The château gardens

Herbs, soft fruit and what the terraced beds give up. The cherry and plum trees below the formal gardens crop in their own time and the kitchen works around them.

The mountain

Pyrenean cheese

Tomme des Pyrénées and the sheep's milk cheeses of the Ariège, from the high pastures above the valley.

The region

What the Ariège is known for

Cassoulet, charcuterie, foie gras, magret de canard, and the mountain hams. Little of it refined, all of it good.

The Source

There Is a Cookbook

An eighteenth-century recipe book belonging to the château survives, and the kitchen still cooks from it.

It is not a museum piece kept behind glass. Dishes once served in this building are made in it again — rustic eighteenth-century cooking alongside the French classics, which is a stranger and better thing than a tasting menu.

One afternoon the plan to cook from it was abandoned in favour of something better. Two women had come up from a village of forty-nine people nearby. One had run the local restaurant. The other's mother had been the cook at Gudanes.

The Cellar

Ariège Wine, Nearly Lost

I

The Romans planted it

Vines have grown in the Ariège since Roman times.

II

The monks developed it

Through the Middle Ages the monasteries did the serious work on it.

III

Phylloxera, then two wars

The vine aphid took most of it in the nineteenth century. The two World Wars took the men who would have replanted. Viticulture here was effectively finished.

IV

Back since the 1990s

Fine wine has only been made in the Ariège again for about thirty years — and most of it is organic. What is poured at this table is largely from producers who restarted something that had stopped.

It is a department that runs quite wild and free.

Karina Waters, on the Ariège

Chef de Cuisine

Tom owned his own restaurant for more than twenty years before he came here, and it was recognised by the Michelin Guide throughout.

He cooks classically and seasonally, which at this altitude means the menu is decided by what the Sunday market and the château gardens give that week rather than by what a card says. Herbs, flowers and produce come from twenty metres away.

He is one of the few chefs equally serious about the savoury and the sweet, which is why the afternoon tea here is not an afterthought. Ask him about French cooking and you will get the history as well as the method.

Why it mattersCooking for one table means he cooks for a room rather than a service. Dietary requirements sent early are designed into the menu, not worked around.
