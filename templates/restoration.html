How the work is governed

Nothing here begins with a decision. It begins with a dossier.

Because the château is Class I listed, every intervention is applied for and approved by the Monuments Historiques before a tool is lifted. Restorers work alongside artisans from the valley. New work is done so that it could one day be removed without harming what was there.

The aim is not to return the château to a single moment in time. It is to let it go on.

A restoration, not a renovation

Restoration answers for every decision: each intervention approved by the Monuments Historiques before a tool is lifted, overpaint lifted by hand, original stone and timber conserved rather than replaced. It is the slower and more exacting course, and the only one worth taking with a house of this consequence.

Class I Historic Monument

Nothing here is done quickly

Gudanes carries the same classification as the Palace of Versailles and the Eiffel Tower. Every intervention — every one, however small — must be applied for and approved by France's Monuments Historiques before a tool is lifted.

Months of recording, investigation and documentation go into each submission. The restoration team itself must include Monuments Historiques architects and artisans, working alongside professional restorers drawn from France, Italy and England.

It is why the walls stay as the centuries left them. Not neglect, and not an aesthetic choice — the law.

Discoveries

What the Château Has Given Up

A restoration this slow turns up things a renovation would have destroyed.

Frescoes Under Cream Paint

Eighteenth-century frescoes found beneath layers of plain cream emulsion in the dining room, lifted back millimetre by millimetre. Guests on longer stays work on them alongside the restoration team.

Erased Coats of Arms

Family crests on the beams were scratched out and painted over during the Revolution, when an aristocratic mark was a dangerous thing to own. The paint conceals them still; the outlines are visible if you know where to look.

A Hole Beneath the Floor

Under rotten ground-floor boards, a deep shaft the contractors believe may have been dug as an escape tunnel during the Second World War. It has never been fully explained.

Gudanes is a Class I Historic Monument, which means the walls stay as the centuries left them — bare plaster, weathered stone, protected by law and not ours to change. Everything within them is new: antique French beds dressed in vintage monogrammed linen, floors relaid by hand, bathrooms finished to a standard the rest of the château has not reached yet. The imperfection is deliberate, and it is the whole argument of this page.

2011

How It Began

01

A drive into the Pyrénées

A taxation accountant and a neurological surgeon from Perth, looking for a house in France. A French friend had expressly forbidden them from buying three things: a historic monument, a château, or a restoration project.

02

Four rooms out of ninety-four

The rest were unreachable — no floors, no ceilings, water through everything. "All we could do was peek around the corners to see what was almost all rubble." Trees were growing inside the building and on the roof.

03

They bought it anyway

"It was like falling in love with an echo from another time and another place. Reality lost focus and our imaginations took hold." Two years of negotiation followed. The contract was signed in March 2013.

04

A four-man tent

Work began that November with a team of five local men. For a period there was nowhere habitable to sleep, and Karina slept outside in a tent.

05

The gates opened

Guests have been welcomed every year since 2016. Nothing leaves the estate. It goes back into the building.

You get to a certain point in your life where you think — you're a long time dead.

Karina Waters, on why they did it

The Law

What Class I Actually Means

Classification is not a plaque. It is a legal instrument, and it governs every decision made in this building.

Approval

Nothing is done unasked

Every intervention is submitted to France's Monuments Historiques and approved before a tool is lifted. Not the finish — the intervention itself.

Waiting

Years, sometimes

One room's painted beams and walls sat untouched for years awaiting permission. The room was decorated and used exactly as it was in the meantime.

Materials

Lime, not cement

Original materials or nothing. Lime render, not modern plaster; the right stone, cut the right way. A building this old has to breathe.

Reversibility

Repair, never replace

What fails is repaired. What cannot be repaired is left visible as a repair rather than disguised as original.

No lift

The staircases stay

A lift cannot be cut into this building. The original stairs are the only way up, and they always will be.

The company

Versailles and the Eiffel Tower

The same designation, and the same obligations. Classified 14 June 1994, reference PA00132943.

We need to learn to slowly adapt ourselves to the château, rather than forcibly demand she adapt to us.

Karina Waters

The Principle

Wabi-Sabi

The idea the whole restoration is built on, and the reason the walls stay as they are.

Wabi

Rustic simplicity. Quietness. Understated elegance — including the quirks and anomalies that come out of how a thing was built, and which make it particular.

Sabi

The beauty that comes with age, when the life of an object and its impermanence show in its patina, its wear, and in any repair left visible.

She will be restored, but her rawness, wear and history will not be erased — instead they will be integrated.

Karina Waters

2011 — 2013

How She Was Found

She had been empty since 1850, on the market four years, and owned by a foreign syndicate who had meant to make her a hotel. The plan had been abandoned.

Of ninety-four rooms, four could be walked into. The rest had caved-in roofs and trees growing through them. There was no power, no plumbing and no water. Negotiations ran two years; the contract was signed in March 2013 and the first tools went in that November.

It wasn't habitable. There was no power, plumbing, water.

Karina Waters, on the château as bought

The Discoveries

What Was Behind the Plaster

Almost none of this was known when the château was bought. It has come out of the walls one collapse at a time.

Renaissance

A hand-painted beamed ceiling

The plaster ceiling of one room came down with an almighty crash and revealed the original painted Renaissance beams behind it. More plaster fell from the wall and took frescoes with it. The room had been dismissed as having no historical value.

18th century

Painted walls under panelling

Eighteenth-century painted walls found beneath later wooden panelling, and Empire wallpaper still on the walls of other rooms.

1789

Revolutionary wax seals

Wax seals from the French Revolution, still on centuries-old walnut doors.

Unexplained

A tunnel going down

A deep shaft leading below ground. Contractors believe it may have been dug as an escape route during the Second World War. It has not been fully explored.

Domestic

Venetian glass and bones

Fragments of Venetian glassware, old ceramics and pottery, and animal bones — the ordinary leavings of three hundred years of household.

1960s

A school holiday camp

One room turned out to have been a shower block when the château was used as a holiday camp. The frescoes were underneath it.

Underfoot

What You Are Walking On

Several floors had collapsed entirely. What went back is not a reproduction — it is the same stone, lifted, kept, and laid again over everything a modern house needs.

The order matters. A concrete slab goes down first, because the joists that held the old floors had rotted through. Heating pipes go into that. Only then does the original stone return.

The result is a room that is warm underfoot in February and looks exactly as it did in 1750. Terracotta and stone were always good at this — they hold heat from a hearth and stay cool in August, which is why the region used them for three hundred years before anyone invented underfloor heating.

The staircase

The original stone, put back

“Recently we put back the original stone tiles.” The grand central staircase, relaid with the tiles that were always on it.

Terracotta

Tomettes

Hexagonal terracotta, the flooring of southern France. The fallen laundry floor was rebuilt and tiled with the original terracotta, lifted and kept.

The salons

Parquet, where it survived

The front salons kept their floors and ceilings. Where a corner of parquet had rotted it is being replaced in kind, not sheeted over.

Flagstone

Laid over heat

Rooms where the floor had fallen in and mushrooms were growing now have a poured slab, underfloor heating, and the original flagstones returned above it.

1970s

Concrete stairs

One set of stairs was put in by the French government in the 1970s. They are not original, they are not pretty, and they are staying — removing them is not the point.

Twelve and twelve

Who laid them

A Catalonian construction team came up from Barcelona. Twelve men rebuilt the floors, walls and ceilings over twelve months, working through the snow.
