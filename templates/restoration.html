Since 2013

Fifteen years in, and not finished

A Class I Historic Monument that was very nearly lost.

  • 1741Ange-Jacques Gabriel, First Architect to Louis XV and the hand behind the Petit Trianon and the Place de la Concorde, designs the château for the Marquis de Gudanes. Work runs to 1750.
  • 1850The last of its residents leave.
  • 1994Classified a Historic Monument on 14 June, the same protected class as the Palace of Versailles and the Eiffel Tower. The status saves it, and then freezes it.
  • 2013Karina and Craig Waters, from Perth, buy a château they have barely seen. Most of the ninety-four rooms cannot be entered. The roof has gone, taking five storeys with it in places. Trees are growing inside the rooms.
  • 2014The first winter Karina stays inside there is no electricity, no heating and no water. She pitches a tent in one of the rooms to keep warm.
  • Eight monthsSpent clearing more than 500 tonnes of fallen stone, debris and rubble before restoration could begin.
  • 2016The Fondation Prince Louis de Polignac awards the restoration its Medal of Honour, presented by Prince Albert II of Monaco. The château opens to guests for the first time.
  • NowRooms are finished one at a time. Cream overpaint is lifted by hand to find frescoes hidden for generations. Wherever possible the original stonework, timber and detail is kept rather than replaced.

How the work is governed

Nothing here begins with a decision. It begins with a dossier.

Because the château is Class I listed, every intervention is applied for and approved by the Monuments Historiques before a tool is lifted. Restorers work alongside artisans from the valley. New work is done so that it could one day be removed without harming what was there.

The aim is not to return the château to a single moment in time. It is to let it go on.

A restoration, not a renovation

Restoration answers for every decision: each intervention approved by the Monuments Historiques before a tool is lifted, overpaint lifted by hand, original stone and timber conserved rather than replaced. It is the slower and more exacting course, and the only one worth taking with a house of this consequence.

2013

Four Rooms of Ninety-Four

This is a restoration, not a renovation — fifteen years in, and years from finished.

When the house was bought in 2013, after two years of negotiation, four of its ninety-four rooms were usable. The rest had caved-in roofs and trees growing through them. Nobody had lived here since 1850.

The previous owners — a foreign syndicate — had planned to cut it into seventeen apartments. France's Monuments Historiques blocked the project, and the dispute left the building to rot for the better part of two decades.

Five hundred tonnes of fallen stone and rubble were cleared before any work could begin. Electricity and plumbing were brought to the house for the first time in living memory.

Everything found along the way has been kept — doorknobs, roof tiles, hinges, floorboards. A dining table was built from trees the wind brought down on the grounds.

Class I Historic Monument

Nothing here is done quickly

Gudanes carries the same classification as the Palace of Versailles and the Eiffel Tower. Every intervention — every one, however small — must be applied for and approved by France's Monuments Historiques before a tool is lifted.

Months of recording, investigation and documentation go into each submission. The restoration team itself must include Monuments Historiques architects and artisans, working alongside professional restorers drawn from France, Italy and England.

It is why the walls stay as the centuries left them. Not neglect, and not an aesthetic choice — the law.

Discoveries

What the House Has Given Up

A restoration this slow turns up things a renovation would have destroyed.

Frescoes Under Cream Paint

Eighteenth-century frescoes found beneath layers of plain cream emulsion in the dining room, lifted back millimetre by millimetre. Guests on longer stays work on them alongside the restoration team.

Erased Coats of Arms

Family crests on the beams were scratched out and painted over during the Revolution, when an aristocratic mark was a dangerous thing to own. The paint conceals them still; the outlines are visible if you know where to look.

A Hole Beneath the Floor

Under rotten ground-floor boards, a deep shaft the contractors believe may have been dug as an escape tunnel during the Second World War. It has never been fully explained.

Eight Hundred Years

Before Any of This

  • 1213First recorded here — a fortification among the holdings of the Counts of Foix. The site sheltered Cathars fleeing the Inquisition; Alesta and Serena de Château-Verdun left for Lombardy rather than recant.
  • 1580Besieged and all but demolished during the Wars of Religion. It stood in ruins for a hundred and sixty years.
  • 1741Louis Gaspard de Sales — Marquis, master of forges, and known as the King of the Pyrénées — commissions Ange-Jacques Gabriel, principal architect to Louis XV and the hand behind the Petit Trianon. Villagers were placed under corvée to build it.
  • 1750Finished, and quickly nicknamed the Versailles of the Pyrénées. De Sales entertained the intellectual and artistic elite here. Voltaire among them.
  • 1789Survived the Revolution where most did not, and passed to a local family. Empire wallpaper from those years is still on some walls.
  • 1850The last year anyone lived here.
  • 1994Classified a Class I Historic Monument on 14 June, alongside Versailles and the Eiffel Tower.
  • 2013Bought by the Waters family and the restoration begins. It has no end date.
  • 2016Awarded the Medal of Honour of the Fondation Prince Louis de Polignac.

Gudanes is a Class I Historic Monument, which means the walls stay as the centuries left them — bare plaster, weathered stone, protected by law and not ours to change. Everything within them is new and genuinely good: the beds, the linen, the floors underfoot, the bathrooms, the food. The imperfection is deliberate, and staying here pays for the next room.
