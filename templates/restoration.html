Since 2013

Fifteen years in, and not finished

A Class I Historic Monument that was very nearly lost.

  • 1741Ange-Jacques Gabriel, First Architect to Louis XV and the hand behind the Petit Trianon and the Place de la Concorde, designs the château for the Marquis de Gudanes. Work runs to 1750.
  • 1850The last of its residents leave.
  • 1994Classified a Historic Monument on 14 June, the same protected class as the Palace of Versailles and the Eiffel Tower. The status saves it, and then freezes it.
  • 2013Karina and Craig Waters, from Perth, buy a château they have barely seen. Most of the ninety-four rooms cannot be entered. The roof has gone, taking five storeys with it in places. Trees are growing inside the rooms.
  • 2014The first winter Karina stays inside there is no electricity, no heating and no water. She pitches a tent in one of the rooms to keep warm.
  • Eight monthsSpent clearing more than 500 tonnes of fallen stone, debris and rubble before restoration could begin.
  • 2016The Fondation Prince Louis de Polignac awards the restoration its Medal of Honour, presented by Prince Albert II of Monaco. The château opens to guests for the first time.
  • NowRooms are finished one at a time. Cream overpaint is lifted by hand to find frescoes hidden for generations. Wherever possible the original stonework, timber and detail is kept rather than replaced.

How the work is governed

Nothing here begins with a decision. It begins with a dossier.

Because the château is Class I listed, every intervention is applied for and approved by the Monuments Historiques before a tool is lifted. Restorers work alongside artisans from the valley. New work is done so that it could one day be removed without harming what was there.

The aim is not to return the château to a single moment in time. It is to let it go on.

A restoration, not a renovation

Restoration answers for every decision: each intervention approved by the Monuments Historiques before a tool is lifted, overpaint lifted by hand, original stone and timber conserved rather than replaced. It is the slower and more exacting course, and the only one worth taking with a house of this consequence.
