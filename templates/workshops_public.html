Château de Gudanes

Château Workshops

Three ways to spend time inside a working restoration — raw, unfinished, and utterly alive — mixed with the real luxury of French food, antiques and craft.

Three lengths, and each one leads with something different. This is not the same holiday sold at three durations — a long weekend here is the brocantes, five days is the kitchen, and a week is the restoration itself. What you come for decides how long you stay.

A château is not a hotel that happens to be old. There is no reception desk, no lift, and no evening turndown. There is a house that has been standing since 1741, four of whose ninety-four rooms were habitable when the family bought it, and a decade of work since to bring back the rest — approved intervention by approved intervention, because France does not allow a Class I monument to be renovated in the ordinary sense.

What that means for a guest is unusual. You are not visiting a finished thing. Bare plaster and weathered stone are protected and will stay; the beds, the linen, the floors and the bathrooms are new and are very good indeed. Both facts are true at once, and neither is an apology.

Meals matter here more than the schedule does. The chefs cook from the Sunday market in the village square, from producers they have used for years, and in summer from the château's own gardens. Breakfast runs long. Lunch is often outside under the lime trees. Dinner is by candlelight at one table, because there is only one table.

Between the working hours there is a pool below the formal gardens, a tennis court in the trees, six acres of parkland to walk at any hour, and the salons — the drawing room, the music room, the two dining rooms — to sit in and do nothing at all. Electricity is limited by design. In the evening the rooms are lit by candle.

Never more than fifteen guests, and the house takes no others while a workshop runs. Full board, château beverages, every activity and the transfers from Toulouse are included in one price.
