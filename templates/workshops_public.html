Noël and the artisan atelier stand apart — one seasonal, one built entirely around making.

A château is not a hotel that happens to be old. There is no reception desk, no lift, and no turndown service. There is a house that has been standing since 1741, four of whose ninety-four rooms were habitable when the family bought it, and a decade of work since to bring back the rest — approved intervention by approved intervention, because France does not allow a Class I monument to be renovated in the ordinary sense.

What that means for a guest is unusual. You are not visiting a finished thing. Bare plaster and weathered stone are protected and will stay; the beds, the linen, the floors and the bathrooms are new and are very good indeed. Both facts are true at once, and neither is an apology.

Meals matter here more than the schedule does. The chefs cook from the Sunday market in the village square, from producers they have used for years, and in summer from the château's own gardens. Breakfast runs long. Lunch is often outside under the lime trees. Dinner is by candlelight in the dining room.

Between the working hours there is a pool below the formal gardens, a tennis court in the trees, six acres of parkland to walk at any hour, and the salons — the drawing room, the music room, the two dining rooms — to sit in and do nothing at all. Electricity is limited by design. In the evening the rooms are lit by candle.

Never more than fifteen guests, and the château takes no others while an atelier runs. Full board, château beverages, every activity and the transfers from Toulouse are included in one price.

Dining

À Table

Candlelight, the fire lit in winter, and whatever the valley gave up that morning.

Every meal is cooked in the château's own Renaissance kitchen, still hung with the copper that came with the château. There is no fixed menu on any atelier — what appears depends on the Sunday market in the village square, on producers the chef has bought from for twenty years, and in summer on the gardens below the terrace.

Breakfast runs long. Lunch is often outside under the lime trees, or packed and carried up the valley. Dinner is by candlelight in the dining room, the whole group together.

Mountain cheeses from the farms above the village, charcuterie, foie gras, magret de canard, wild mushrooms when the season gives them, and desserts drawn from a recipe book written at Gudanes in the 1700s. A private selection of local wines, spirits and soft drinks is poured with every meal and included in the price.

Bathing

Les Salles de Bains

Floral papers, a cast-iron freestanding bath, and a dressing room to yourself.

Three on the ground floor, each papered differently — brass fittings, hexagonal terracotta underfoot, deep towels, and a window onto the valley. They are the most recently finished rooms in the building, and it shows.

Ensuite bathrooms are not permitted under historic preservation rules, which is why they sit together downstairs rather than off each bedroom. It also means they are among the most modern rooms in the building. On a workshop they are shared between the group and privately allocated.

Cooking in the Cuisine

The Market Comes First

The kitchen does not write a menu and then go shopping. It goes to the market and then decides.

Les Cabannes on a Sunday, three minutes below the gates — a village of three hundred and seventeen people. Tarascon-sur-Ariège on Wednesday and Saturday, twenty minutes down the valley and much larger. La Ferme du Quié in the village for wine, cheese, ham and whatever else it has that week.

You go with the chef, who has bought from the same producers for twenty years and will tell you which stall is worth queueing at. Then it comes back and you cook it — including, on some days, from the château's own eighteenth-century recipe book.

Une journée d'atelierA day in the atelier

Hour by Hour

What a Day Actually Looks Like

  1. 8.00 Breakfast in the dining salon. Long, and nobody rushes it.
  2. 9.30 Into the room you are working on. Overalls provided; wear what you would not mind ruining underneath.
  3. 13.00 Lunch, usually outside. Wine if you want it, though the afternoon is fiddly.
  4. 14.30 Back to it until about six. A good afternoon is measured in centimetres.
  5. 20.00 Dinner together at one table. This is the part people write about afterwards.

PhysicalYou are on your feet, often on a platform, often with your arms above your head. Say if that is a problem and we will place you where it is not.

Before You Come

What to Bring, and What Not To

Bring

  • Clothes you would not mind ruining — lime does not wash out
  • Shoes with a closed toe and a flat sole
  • Reading glasses if you use them. The work is close
  • A notebook. Most people want one by day two

Do not

  • Bring tools. Everything is here, and ours are sharpened
  • Expect to finish something. You are joining work in progress
  • Book if you cannot manage stairs — the ateliers are not on one floor
  • Come for a demonstration. You will be doing it

No experienceNone is needed. Half of every atelier has never held a trowel, and the artisans are used to it.

Une session entièreA whole session

For Institutions

Taking a Session Outright

A session holds fifteen. A school, a conservation course or a heritage body can take all fifteen at once, which is one conversation rather than fifteen.

The work is real: eighteenth-century frescoes under supervision, lime plaster, gilding, joinery. Students are not shown a demonstration — they lift overpaint from a Class I Historic Monument, and what they do stays on the wall.

What a cohort gets

  • All fifteen places on one session, dates agreed with you
  • The château to yourselves for the duration — no other guests
  • Taught by the artisans restoring this building, not by visiting tutors
  • Beds for fifteen; the village takes any overflow
  • All meals, cooked here, including the market morning

Who it suits

  • Conservation and restoration courses
  • Architecture and fine-art schools
  • Heritage bodies and building-trust members
  • Craft guilds and professional development groups
  • Museum and gallery teams

Documentation for course credit can be arranged; ask early, not late.
